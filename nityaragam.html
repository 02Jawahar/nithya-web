Nityaragam

Music that never stops — a blog my mother started years before I was born, now an initiative to make Carnatic music accessible to Gen Z.

My mother trained in Carnatic vocal and veena as a child in Visakhapatnam, under a teacher whose own lineage traced back through some of the tradition’s most respected names. She kept training long after she left India — I’ve heard the story more times than I can count of her driving 85 miles each way, alone, for a single weekly veena lesson, just to keep it going. Music followed her through marriage, through raising kids, through loss. It never once stopped being nitya — eternal — to her, even when everything else in her life changed shape.

I inherited that, whether I meant to or not. When I was eight, I remember dreading practice. By eighth grade, something shifted: my mother asked me to build my own swara kalpana — my own note-patterns inside a raga, ascending and descending — and for the first time, I wasn’t just repeating what I’d been taught. I was making something. I still remember being amazed at how many genuinely different patterns I could find inside one raga’s rules, and even more amazed that musicians for generations before me had worked all of this out by hand, on paper, with nothing but an ear for structure and a lot of patience.

That’s the real inheritance, I think — not the notes themselves, but the discipline underneath them.

My mother handed me a structure she’d spent her whole life inside of, and trusted me to find my own way through it. Everything I’ve made since, in music and outside it, has started from that same instinct: learn a structure completely enough that you can find every honest path through it.

Music was never going to stop with her. I don’t expect it to stop with me either.

The initiative today

I’ve taken over Nityaragam with one objective: making Carnatic music accessible to Gen Z — the same tradition, told in a language my generation actually reads.
